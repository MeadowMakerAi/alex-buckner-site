I started the Sweet Plum packaging study with a photograph and a flavor name. Changing the pack from a pouch to a round tin meant cropping the image, adding a lid and giving someone on the line another job.

The flavor and the pack

In the original Peach layout, I gave the crowd photograph most of the front. The wordmark and the orange flavor strip sit beside it. For Sweet Plum I kept that arrangement and changed the strip to a muted plum, so the flavor name stays easy to find.

A pouch gives me a rectangle. On the round lid, I have to decide which faces to keep as the corners of that rectangle fall away. I also need to keep the flavor name clear of the rim and leave room on the pack for information that changes with each batch.

From the mold to the packing table

I used the wider Meadow Maker gummy production plan to trace the route to packing. In that plan the gummies start with a pectin base. The depositor fills silicone molds. Once the pieces have set, they come out of the molds and go onto trays to cure. The molds can go back for cleaning. The trays are still occupied when the next batch arrives.

I used the floor model to follow a worker carrying a load. In one proposed layout, the cooker blocked the route. That layout failed the simulation. I could rule it out before buying or moving a machine.

What changes at the packing table

I’d test the pouch first. For the second run I’d put a filled tin inside an outer pouch. Keeping the pouch in both runs lets me measure the time spent handling the tin and see whether it protects more pieces.

The cost of a finished pack

I can get a price for an empty tin. To know what it costs to add one, I also need the packing time, the damaged pieces and the packs someone has to open and do again.

I work back from the shelf price through the retailer’s margin and distribution. That tells me what the business can spend on each pack. In FM Command, I keep the depositor and tin-filling quotes with the gummy project so I can trace those assumptions to their sources.

The first packing trial

I’d split one cured batch between the two formats, use the same count and packing table, and time each run through inspection. After the same handling, I’d open samples from each run and count damaged pieces.

I’d keep the pouch on its own unless the tin protects enough pieces to justify its cost. For the artwork check, I’d photograph the pouch at shelf distance and the tin as someone first sees it after opening the pouch.
